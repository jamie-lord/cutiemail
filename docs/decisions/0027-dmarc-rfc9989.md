# 0027. Which half of RFC 9989 this server implements

## Status

Accepted.

## Context

RFC 9989 (May 2026) obsoletes RFC 7489, the document every line of DMARC in this repository was
written against. It is not a clarifying revision. Four things change that a receiver can observe:

1. **Policy discovery.** 7489 §6.6.3 queries `_dmarc.<Author Domain>` and, failing that, jumps
   straight to the Public Suffix List organizational domain. 9989 §4.10 replaces that with a DNS
   tree walk up the name, one label at a time.
2. **The organizational domain itself** is no longer a PSL lookup. It is whichever name in the walk
   published a record, disambiguated by a new `psd` tag, and §4.10.2 requires a separate walk per
   authenticated identifier when checking alignment.
3. **`pct` is gone**, retired to historic in Appendix A.6 and in the IANA registry, replaced by a
   `t` test-mode tag that demotes the policy one level rather than sampling a share of failures.
4. **`np`** applies a distinct policy to non-existent subdomains.

The PSL jump is not merely dated; it loses policy that domain owners have published. For an Author
Domain of `alerts.corp.example.com` where `corp.example.com` publishes `p=reject` and the apex
publishes nothing, 7489 discovery looks at the Author Domain, then `example.com`, finds neither, and
applies no policy at all. The record exists, in DNS, saying reject, and this server delivered the
message to the inbox.

That same gap runs the other way and reaches our own operators. The deployment guide recommends a
mail domain below the registered domain (`you@mail.example.com`), and under 7489 the `_dmarc` record
`setup` generates for it governs exactly that one name — a receiver evaluating
`billing@anything.mail.example.com` skips it and consults `_dmarc.example.com` instead. 9989's walk
visits the intermediate name and finds ours. The receivers deciding today are still the ones that
matter, so `doctor` gained a `dmarc-org` check for the apex record as well; the two fixes address the
same hole from opposite ends and neither replaces the other.

Adopting 9989 wholesale is a different size of job. §4.10.2 alignment means up to eight queries for
the Author Domain plus eight for each DKIM `d=` and the SPF domain, on an unauthenticated path, per
message. `np` needs a non-existent-domain determination this server does not make. `psd` needs the
walk to continue past the registered domain to the TLD. None of that is hard in isolation; together
it is a rewrite of the DNS layer of DMARC, and it buys least where this server runs.

## Decision

**Adopt policy discovery and test mode. Keep the Public Suffix List for alignment. Implement neither
`psd` nor `np`.**

**Discovery walks the ancestors, floored at the PSL organizational domain** (`policyAncestors` in
`server/dmarc-inbound.ts`). A record at the Author Domain still wins outright and still costs one
query. Otherwise the walk queries the organizational domain first and works downward, taking the
first hit.

Downward is not an optimisation, it is the specification. §4.10.2 selects "the DMARC Policy Record
found at the name with the fewest number of labels", so where both an intermediate name and the apex
publish, the *shallowest* record governs — the opposite of the intuition that the most specific one
should. Walking down and stopping at the first hit computes that selection directly, and it means the
ordinary two-label sender costs the same two lookups it always did.

The floor is what buys the simplicity. Never walking above the registered domain means no `psd` tag
to interpret, no way to apply a public suffix operator's policy to a domain that never asked for one,
and a bound that falls out of the PSL rather than needing its own rule. §4.10's own cap still
applies on top: an Author Domain of eight or more labels jumps to its seven-label suffix, so a From
header of two hundred labels cannot buy two hundred DNS queries from an unauthenticated peer.

The change is close to monotone. Every message that gets a policy under 7489 gets the same policy
here, because the organizational domain is queried first and wins when it publishes. What changes is
that a record published *between* the Author Domain and the organizational domain is applied where
previously it was ignored — in either direction, since the intermediate name may as easily publish
`p=none` as `p=reject`. That is the domain owner's stated preference either way.

**`t=y` demotes the governing policy one level** (§4.7): reject becomes quarantine, quarantine
becomes none. `checkDmarc` returns the *demoted* policy as `policy` and the undemoted one separately
as `publishedPolicy`, which is for the `Authentication-Results` trace and nothing else. There is
deliberately no field the enforcement path could pick wrongly: the one value it reads is already
correct, and honouring test mode required no change at the enforcement site at all.

Only a literal `y` demotes. §4.7 makes `n` the default, so a garbled value fails towards enforcing
the published policy rather than silently disarming it.

**`pct` stays.** It is historic in 9989 and still published in quantity by senders who wrote their
records against 7489. Dropping it would enforce in full against domains that asked for a sample,
which is the same over-enforcement that ignoring `t` would cause, just aimed at the other era's
senders. Honouring both costs one boolean and one comparison.

**`np` and `psd` are not added to the parser's known-tag set.** They are real 9989 tags, but listing
a tag whose semantics we ignore would claim otherwise; leaving them unknown-and-ignored is both
honest and what §6.3 requires. A published `np` falls through to the `sp`/`p` default.

## Consequences

- A DMARC policy published at an intermediate name is now enforced. On a personal deployment the
  most likely place to notice is mail from large organisations with per-department subdomains.
- Discovery can cost up to eight DNS queries where it previously cost two, but only for an Author
  Domain several labels below its registered domain. The common cases — a record at the Author
  Domain, or one label below it — are unchanged at one and two queries.
- A domain part-way through a DMARC rollout, publishing its target policy with `t=y`, now reaches the
  inbox instead of Junk. This is the change most likely to be mistaken for a regression, which is why
  the trace reads `dmarc=fail (p=quarantine t=y)` rather than a bare `p=none` that would look as
  though the zone had said nothing.
- Alignment still compares PSL organizational domains. Where 9989's per-identifier tree walk would
  disagree — a sender publishing records at intermediate names specifically to *split* alignment
  between departments — this server aligns them where 9989 would not. That is a real divergence and
  it is not fixed by anything above. **Revisit** if a legitimate sender is ever observed relying on
  it, or if the PSL's refresh cadence becomes the thing that breaks alignment.
- Multiple published records at any one name still terminate discovery with no policy applied, per
  7489 §6.6.3 step 5; 9989's walk continues past them. This can only matter once the organizational
  domain has been shown to publish nothing, so it cannot lose a policy 7489 would have applied. It is
  a deviation all the same, and `doctor` now reports the case for our own zone.
- `spec/rfc9989.txt` is vendored alongside `spec/rfc7489.txt`. Both stay: the alignment and grammar
  requirements are still cited to 7489, which is what the deployed senders published against.

## Consequences for the register

Six requirements added, all cited to `rfc9989`: `R-9989-4.10-a` (the eight-query bound),
`R-9989-4.10.1-a` (walk when the Author Domain has no record), `R-9989-4.10.1-b` (`sp` governs a
record found above the Author Domain), `R-9989-4.10.2-a` (fewest labels wins), `R-9989-4.7-a`
(test mode) and `R-9989-11.5-a` (evaluate every author domain, strictest failing policy wins). The
7489 grammar and alignment entries are unchanged.

The last of those was added by a later audit rather than with the rest, and the gap it closed is
worth naming: this server already treated more than one author mailbox as never-authentic, and
already forced such a message to `fail`. What it did not do was pick the policy on any principle the
attacker could not override — it evaluated a single mailbox, and the extractor takes the LAST one, so
appending a policy-less domain of their own left the victim's address displayed first and the failure
unenforced. Detecting a spoof and then not acting on it is worse than either alone, because the trace
says `dmarc=fail` while the message sits in the inbox. §11.5 also warns that evaluating every domain
is itself a denial-of-service vector, so both the domain count and the total lookups per message are
capped.

`RequirementDef.page` became optional. The RFC Editor's current text format has no page breaks —
RFC 9989 carries zero `[Page N]` markers where RFC 7489 carries 73 — so for those documents the
section number is the only locator, and a page number would have been a figure no reader could check
against the vendored file.
